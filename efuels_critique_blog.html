§ 01 — Introduction

A paper born from ambition, not engineering

The Paris Agreement set a deadline, not a methodology. In the decade since, a peculiar category of research has proliferated: studies that demonstrate thermodynamic permission for a process — that nature does not forbid it — and then present this as evidence of industrial viability. The paper under review, "The Potential of CO₂ Hydrogenation to Produce e-Fuels" (Amorim, Faria & Rocha, Energy & Fuels, 2026), sits squarely in this category.

To be clear about what the paper does and does not do: it simulates an equilibrium reactor in Aspen Plus V14, sweeps temperature, pressure, and H₂/CO₂ ratios across 12 potential e-fuels, computes a rentability parameter α, and — for the three cases where α exceeds 1 — constructs a simplified cash flow model. The authors are transparent about what they omit: no heat integration, no recycle streams, no catalyst costs, no purification. They frame the work as a "preliminary screening study."

That framing is fair. What is less fair is the paper's title, which promises a techno-economic analysis, and its abstract, which highlights that "gasoline shows potential rentability under both best- and worst-case conditions." By the time a reader reaches Table 10 — buried on page 10 — they find that e-gasoline must sell at 52% above conventional gasoline prices to break even, and e-diesel at 142% above fossil diesel. That is the real finding. Everything else is scaffolding.

§ 02 — Paper Overview

What the study set out to do

Scope: Twelve e-fuels (methane through diesel, methanol, ethanol, butanol, DME) evaluated for thermodynamic feasibility and economic viability via CO₂ hydrogenation using green H₂. Simulated in Aspen Plus V14 using an RGibbs equilibrium reactor under varying T (150–450 °C), P (1–100 bar), and H₂/CO₂ ratios (1–5). A subsequent integrated flowsheet added compression, heat exchange, and a flash separator to estimate OPEX, then CAPEX for the three α > 1 cases.

The study's comparative intent is legitimate. Running every fuel through the same flowsheet under consistent assumptions produces a fair relative ranking. Gasoline emerging as the most promising e-fuel candidate is consistent with its high market price (€2.09/kg) and moderate H₂ stoichiometry. The sensitivity analysis linking profitability to a halving of hydrogen costs — aligned with 2050 green hydrogen price forecasts — is the paper's genuinely useful contribution.

§ 03 — Technical Critique

Six faults that undermine the analysis

Each omission below is individually defensible as a simplification. Together, they compound into an OPEX and CAPEX estimate that is systematically and substantially too optimistic — meaning every α value in the paper is inflated, and the three cases that clear α > 1 may not do so under realistic assumptions.

  • 01 /

    RGibbs gives a ceiling, not a result

    The Gibbs free energy minimisation reactor assumes the system reaches full thermodynamic equilibrium — the best possible conversion at a given T and P, with infinite residence time and perfect selectivity. Real CO₂ hydrogenation is a kinetically controlled process. Catalyst selectivity, active site coverage, poisoning, and mass transfer limitations all determine what actually comes out of the reactor. Fischer-Tropsch product distributions follow Anderson-Schulz-Flory statistics — a chain-growth probability that favours lighter hydrocarbons — not the Gibbs minimum. For gasoline-range (C₆) and diesel-range (C₉) targets, the gap between equilibrium prediction and real catalyst output is not marginal. It is the entire engineering challenge. Presenting RGibbs outputs as yield estimates in a techno-economic analysis conflates a thermodynamic bound with an engineering outcome.

  • 02 /

    Isothermal reactor — energy cost unaccounted

    The authors assume constant-temperature reactor operation but do not account for the energy cost of maintaining it. The reactions in Table 2 are exothermic, some intensely so (FTS to paraffins, Sabatier at −165 kJ/mol, DME at −123 kJ/mol). Removing this heat requires cooling circuits, intercoolers, or steam generation. In a full OPEX model, reactor thermal management contributes meaningfully to utility costs and can represent significant additional CAPEX for heat exchange area. The paper states that "reactor energy expenses were not considered, as previous techno-economic analysis has shown the energy required is negligible." This justification applies to the energy cost of maintaining temperature in mildly exothermic systems where heat is recovered — not to the gross heat duty of highly exothermic synthesis reactions operated isothermally without heat integration.

  • 03 /

    No recycle streams — hydrogen loss uncosted

    Green hydrogen at €3/kg is the paper's own identified dominant cost driver. Single-pass CO₂ conversion, even at best-case conditions, leaves unreacted hydrogen in the outlet. The paper acknowledges hydrogen recycle was excluded to preserve "comparability" between cases, but this creates a paradox: the economic analysis penalises hydrogen feedstock cost while ignoring that a meaningful fraction of that feedstock exits unreacted. In a real plant, unreacted H₂ must be either recycled (requiring a compressor and separator — additional CAPEX and OPEX) or vented/flared (a cost and an emissions problem). Neither option is free. Excluding recycle also means the α parameter is computed against a hydrogen consumption rate that overstates how much is actually converted to product.

  • 04 /

    Separation modelled as a single flash block

    Product recovery is modelled using a single equilibrium flash separator. In practice, separating a reactor effluent containing C₁–C₉ hydrocarbons, water, CO₂, CO, and unreacted H₂ requires a distillation train, potentially a pressure swing adsorption unit for H₂/CO₂ separation, and fuel-specific finishing steps (hydrotreatment for diesel-range waxes, isomerisation for gasoline octane specification). The paper itself notes that "compatibility with existing motors requires e-gasoline and e-kerosene to respect composition requirements" — a constraint that cannot be met by a flash block. The CAPEX estimate of €1.4–1.5M for three compressors, a reactor, heat exchangers, and a condenser is not a plant cost. It is the cost of a handful of unit operations in a plant that would in reality be an order of magnitude larger.

  • 05 /

    Hydrogen provenance never specified

    The paper sets hydrogen cost at €3/kg — described as "the lower limit of the reported European production cost range" — but never states what process produces this hydrogen, how it is delivered, or what its carbon intensity is. This matters because the entire rationale for e-fuel production is carbon neutrality. If the H₂ is produced by steam methane reforming (gray hydrogen, currently ~€1.5–2/kg), the carbon argument for the e-fuels collapses. If it is electrolytic green hydrogen (currently €3–7/kg in Europe), the economic case is even harder than the paper models. The €3/kg figure appears to be green hydrogen at an optimistic end — but the paper does not state this. A techno-economic analysis that does not pin its feedstock to a production pathway cannot make credible sustainability claims.

  • 06 /

    N₂ dilution — energy cost inconsistently handled

    The integrated approach feeds flue gas (80 vol% N₂, 20 vol% CO₂) through a multi-stage compression train alongside pure H₂. N₂ is correctly excluded from yield calculations as an inert, but its presence in the compression train is real — 200 kmol/h of N₂ at 250 kmol/h total flue gas flow is being compressed from 1 bar to up to 100 bar alongside the reactive species. The compression duty reported implicitly includes compressing this inert load. Yet the OPEX comparison is normalised per kg of e-fuel produced, meaning the compression cost is allocated entirely against productive output, without acknowledgement that a pre-separation step (PSA, membrane, or absorber) to remove N₂ before compression would substantially reduce this duty — but would itself carry CAPEX and OPEX. The current treatment neither models pre-separation nor correctly allocates the N₂ compression penalty.

"Thermodynamics has never been the bottleneck for any industrial process. The question is never can nature permit this — it is always can engineering make it pay." — From this critique
§ 04 — The Missing Engineering

What a complete model would require

Consider what a minimal but defensible e-gasoline plant model looks like versus what the paper models. The gap is not cosmetic:

/* Paper's model */ Feed (CO₂ + H₂ + N₂) → Compressor train ✓ included → Feed heater/cooler ✓ included → RGibbs reactor (isothermal) ✗ no kinetics, no heat duty → Valve (pressure drop) ✓ included → Flash separator ✗ not a distillation column → Product ✓ costed at market price /* Minimum realistic model */ Feed (CO₂ + H₂ + N₂) → N₂/CO₂ pre-separator + CAPEX + OPEX → Compressor train (smaller) lower duty, less cost → Feed heater → Kinetic reactor (non-isothermal) + cooling circuit → Heat recovery (HEN) reduces utility cost → H₂/CO recycle loop + recycle compressor + PSA → CO₂ recycle loop + absorber/compressor → Distillation train C₁/C₂ rejection, C₆ cut → Hydrotreatment / isomerisation fuel spec compliance → Product

The analogy that comes to mind: modelling ammonia synthesis as N₂ + H₂ → NH₃ in a Gibbs reactor, noting that ΔG is negative at high pressure, and concluding that ammonia production is economically viable — without a Haber-Bosch reactor, recycle compressor, or separation column. Thermodynamics permits it. Engineering makes it happen. The two are not the same claim.

§ 05 — The Broader Problem

Publication pressure and net-zero urgency

This paper is not an isolated case. It reflects a structural problem in how net-zero research is being produced and reviewed. The urgency of the 2050 decarbonisation target has created enormous institutional demand for publications demonstrating progress — or at least demonstrating possibility. RGibbs reactor studies are efficient to produce: Aspen Plus has a built-in block, sensitivity analysis is automated, and a twelve-fuel comparison generates enough figures and tables for a full journal article.

The result is a growing literature of what might be called thermodynamic existence proofs — papers that show a reaction pathway is not thermodynamically forbidden, dress the result in economic language, and submit. Peer review does not always catch this, partly because the omissions are declared openly (which makes them appear deliberate) and partly because reviewers in energy economics may not be process engineers, and vice versa.

The cost is real. Research funding, graduate student time, and editorial bandwidth are finite. A preliminary screening study that occupies journal space and cites its own α > 1 finding in the abstract draws attention away from the work that would actually move e-fuels from concept to plant: detailed kinetic modelling, catalyst lifetime studies under industrial conditions, rigorous heat integration design, and full process simulation with recycle. We have until 2050, not 2500. The engineering needs to start now, and it starts by being honest about what Aspen Plus's RGibbs block can and cannot tell us.

§ 06 — Verdict

What this paper contributes, and what it does not

The honest finding of this study is stark: under current hydrogen pricing, no e-fuel produced by CO₂ hydrogenation is economically viable. E-gasoline becomes viable if and only if hydrogen prices fall to €1–2/kg — prices not expected until 2040–2050 — or if e-fuel selling prices far exceed their fossil equivalents. That is a useful, if unsurprising, conclusion. It deserved a clearer headline.

What holds up

  • Consistent comparative framework across 12 fuels
  • Sensitivity to H₂ price is the most policy-relevant finding
  • Thermodynamic trends (T, P, H₂/CO₂ ratio effects) are correctly captured
  • Transparent about omissions — recycle, heat integration, separation
  • Table 10 (minimum selling prices) is the honest result

What does not

  • RGibbs ≠ kinetically realistic reactor — yields are theoretical ceilings
  • Isothermal operation without heat duty is an incomplete OPEX model
  • No H₂ recycle — the dominant cost driver partially exits unreacted
  • Flash separation cannot deliver fuel-specification products
  • CAPEX at €1.4–1.5M excludes most real plant costs
  • H₂ provenance (green vs. gray) never established
